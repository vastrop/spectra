# Carbon star (C*) — molecular carbon dominates instead of oxides

## Class: C/O > 1 flips the molecular chemistry
A carbon star has more carbon than oxygen in its atmosphere (C/O > 1), the reverse of the Sun and of normal K/M giants. Once oxygen is locked into CO, the leftover carbon forms C2, CN and CH instead of the TiO/VO oxide bands that shape M-star spectra at the same temperature. The most familiar carbon stars are cool, intrinsic **C-N AGB giants**, enriched when third dredge-up carries carbon made by helium burning to the surface. Carbon stars are not all intrinsic AGB stars, however: extrinsic stars acquired carbon and s-process material from a former AGB companion, while dwarf carbon stars and hydrogen-deficient carbon stars have different histories.

The revised carbon classification includes cooler classical **C-N** stars; warmer **C-R** stars, broadly comparable to G/K giants; metal-poor, strongly CH- and s-process-enhanced **C-H** stars, generally products of binary mass transfer; **C-J** stars with unusually strong ¹³C features and a low ¹²C/¹³C ratio; and rare hydrogen-deficient **C-Hd** stars. C-J is primarily an isotopic category and can overlap the R-, N- or H-like spectral families. Cool C-N stars are very red and commonly variable, but carbon stars as a whole are not necessarily extremely red or large-amplitude variables. Intrinsic AGB carbon stars may be Mira, semiregular or irregular variables; changes in brightness, molecular-band strength and circumstellar dust can alter the observed continuum between sessions.

## This type: what to expect, and what to rule out
There is no Pickles carbon-star reference in this library — none of the standard templates carry C2/CN chemistry — so a carbon star can only be matched by eye against this card, and **must never be used as a response-calibration reference**: its deep molecular troughs and blue cutoff would carve spurious structure into the derived instrument response.

At R≈100 the principal signature is the **Swan system of C2**: several bandheads sharp on their **red edge and shading off to the blue** (degraded blue), the opposite sense from the red-degraded TiO bands in M stars. Band orientation is a useful clue, but do not classify from one apparent edge alone: require several consistent C2/CN bands and the absence of the expected TiO pattern. Many C-N stars also show a strong **blue/violet flux cutoff**, with little or no flux shortward of ~4400–4500 Å under a forest of CN and C2 absorption, so judge exposure and SNR from the red half of the trace, not the blue. CH is strong in C-R and especially C-H types; in cool C-N stars it is often overwhelmed by CN/C2 opacity. Strong Ba II 4554 Å is evidence of s-process enrichment but is not unique to C-H stars: C-N stars may also show strong Ba, and C-H classification relies on the exceptionally strong CH spectrum, especially its broad P-branch depression redward of the G band, together with the metal-poor, s-process-enhanced character. Strong Li I 6708 Å identifies a lithium-rich carbon star but does not by itself distinguish hot-bottom burning, dredge-up or another enrichment mechanism.

**Rule out the look-alikes.** An **M giant** shows TiO bands degraded to the *red* (shaded blue-to-red, opposite Swan), including the characteristic TiO structure near 7054 Å, but lacks a consistent sequence of blue-degraded Swan heads. An **S star** lies near the C/O ≈ 1 transition and commonly shows **ZrO** with weakened TiO; in the most carbon-rich SC stars TiO and ZrO may both be weak and C2 may begin to appear. Check for ZrO, including the band near 6474 Å, before calling a marginal case carbon. A heavily reddened, dust-obscured late-K giant can mimic the overall red slope but lacks the diagnostic sequence of C2 and CN bands.
